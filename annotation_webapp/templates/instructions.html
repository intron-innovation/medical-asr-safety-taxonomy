📖 Annotation Instructions & Guide

Complete guide to annotating ASR errors in medical conversations

1. Overview

You will annotate safety-related errors in ASR (Automatic Speech Recognition) transcripts of medical conversations. The goal of the project is to assess how safe clinical ASR systems are, beyond the automated approach of WER. For each error you will rate its clinical severity (scale of 1–3) and select the taxonomy category that suggest the reason why the error occurred (taxonomy below). Everything highlighted should mostly be medically relevant words, but many are also ordinary words with no specific clinical meaning.

Your Task:

  • Click on each highlighted error
  • Choose the ASR Error Class: decide from the text. See Section 2, ASR Error Class for details. Most errors can be labelled without the audio. Clicking an error moves the audio player to that part of the recording
  • Rate the clinical severity (Minor / Moderate / Severe): you must select a severity based on the error's potential impact on clinical understanding and patient care. See Section 5, Severity Guidelines.
  • Save to auto-sync to server

Note: a running tracker shows how many errors you've annotated in the current session (e.g. "5 / 10 errors annotated"). A session is marked INCOMPLETE until every flagged error in it has been annotated, and you cannot advance to the next session until it is complete.

2. ASR Error Class (8 Categories)

Classify why the ASR failed. Adapted from Zafar et al. (2004).

Rule 1 — start from the WORD, not the sound. Pronunciation explains nearly every error, so starting from pronunciation will most likely lead to the wrong label. Ask what kind of word was damaged.
[SUB:amlodipine->pain] — wrong: "that's a mispronunciation". Right: "amlodipine is a drug name" → Dictionary error.
Rule 2 — one tag, one label, unless two words were damaged. If the tag covers a single word the form will not let you pick a second. Only a tag spanning two or more damaged words may take more than one label. Example: [SUB:heartbeat shake->beating cheek] is Dictionary (heartbeat→beating) and Annunciation/Homonym (shake→cheek). Never add a second label to be safe.
One exception: a dropped or invented medical word ([DEL:…] / [INS:…]) may take Dictionary and ASR omission / insertion together.

Work down and take the first that fits. Steps 1 to 4 use the text only. You don't need to play the audio for these cases.

1. Suffix error

The same word with only its ending changed: plural, tense or -ing/-ed. This applies to medical words too. If the word class changes (abdominal → abdomen), it is Dictionary.

[SUB:vomit->vomits]  •  [SUB:complaint->complaints]  •  [SUB:swelling->swollen]  •  [SUB:palpitation->palpitations]  •  [SUB:fatigue->fatigued]

2. Dictionary error

The damaged word was a drug, condition, symptom, anatomy term, procedure, or a proper noun / place / name. Words patients use for these count even if informal (achy, weed). Also when a medical word becomes non-medical, or the reverse, and when a medical word is dropped or invented ([DEL:…] / [INS:…]): you may also tick ASR omission / insertion. If only the ending changed, it is Suffix. If the word class changes (abdominal → abdomen), it is Dictionary.

amlodipine → pain  •  naproxen → proxen  •  cetirizine → trazinia  •  ulcer → asthma  •  Ekiti → Nkita  •  abdominal → abdomen  •  [DEL:fatigue]

3. Stop words

Only a short function word changed — a, an, the, is, has, if.

[SUB:a->the]  •  [DEL:a]  •  [INS:in]  •  [SUB:or->so]

4. Nonsense error

The output is not a real word at all, even after trying to guess from context.

wisdom teeth → visimteeth  •  swampy region → iswampimidio  •  feverish → beverish

Still no label? Now play the audio.

The next four labels depend on what was actually said or recorded. Play the audio at this error, then continue down the list.

5. Human transcription error

The transcript is at fault, not the model — the ASR was right. Play the audio. Choose this only when the audio clearly shows the speaker said the ASR’s words and the reference’s words were not said. If the audio is unclear or hard to hear, do not choose this: “hard to hear” is not evidence that the reference is wrong. Pick the ASR-error label instead.
(a) the human typed it wrong
(b) the reference does not match what was said: the human missed speech the speaker said (shows up as an insertion), or typed words nobody said (shows up as a deletion). Ask: did the speaker really say this? If the ASR is the one that dropped or invented it, use ASR omission / insertion.

reference "catherization" → ASR "catheterization"  •  reference "lose" → ASR "loose"  •  [INS:alcohol] said twice, typed once  •  [INS:is there any drug], a question the transcript omits

6. Audio glitch

A fault in the recording at this spot. This includes a dropout, distortion, a click or pop, a cut-off, or a burst of noise over the speech. An accent, or fast, slurred or mumbled speech, is not a glitch. If a glitch hides a medical term, label it Dictionary error instead (steps 1 to 4 come first).

a word cut off mid-syllable  •  a click or dropout where a word should be

7. Annunciation / Homonym error

An ordinary English word was misheard as another word that sounds like it — through accent, speed, slurring, a pause, or plain phonetic similarity. If the damaged word was specialist, use Dictionary error instead. A real word swapped for another real word on an ordinary word is still this label.

nausea → nuisance  •  abdominal pain → abnormal pain  •  pus → pulse  •  fainting → painting  •  worst → worse

8. ASR omission / insertion

[INS:…] or [DEL:…] only. The reference is right and the ASR dropped or invented ordinary words. If the reference is the one at fault, use Human transcription error. A dropped or invented medical word is Dictionary (you may tick both).

[INS:for you]  •  [INS:of july]  •  [DEL:urine]  •  [DEL:okay and do you take any medications i had a a drug history of thyroxine]

Helpful Tips

There could be cases where the error is a medical term but the cause is obvious from how the speaker said the word. In that case, you can use Annunciation / Homonym error if the error is leaning towards this category.

Severity Descriptions

  • Minor: Little or no chance of impacting the health practitioner's understanding of the conversation and/or line of management for the patient.
  • Moderate: Some chance of impacting the health practitioner's understanding of the conversation and/or line of management for the patient.
  • Severe: Likely to lead to an egregious clinical error — possibly omission/inclusion of a key life-impacting detail in the conversation. Reserve severity 3 for errors touching a diagnosis, symptom, medication, procedure, or a negation (e.g. irregularities → regularities flips the meaning and is severity 3).

How to choose the severity

Q1. Does the damaged word carry clinical content? A diagnosis, a symptom, a medication, a procedure or test, a number (dose, duration, frequency), or a negation. If not → 1 Minor.

Q2. If yes, what does the transcript now say?

What the reader gets Severity
Wrong A plausible but different clinical fact. A reader would act on it. 3 Severe
Missing The fact is gone or garbled. A reader would notice and have to ask. 2 Moderate
  • Always 3: a medication replaced or lost, a diagnosis replaced, a negation flipped, a dose or number changed.
  • A long run dropped or invented by the ASR: 5 or more words that carry clinical content is 3. Fillers only is 1.
Stuck between 2 and 3? Ask: would a clinician reading only this transcript believe something false? Yes → 3. No, they would just be missing something → 2.
A practical test for severity: "If this went uncorrected, and if you could only read the transcript alone, would it have changed your understanding of the patient's clinical condition?"

4. Detailed Examples

Severity anchors — calibrate against these

[SUB:amlodipine->pain] — Dictionary error, severity 3: a medication is lost.
[SUB:ulcer->asthma] — Dictionary error, severity 3: one diagnosis becomes another.
[SUB:irregularities->regularities] — Annunciation/Homonym, severity 3: the negation flips.
[SUB:gastrings->gallstones] — Dictionary error, severity 3: a different diagnosis that reads as true.
[SUB:urethra->ureteral] — Dictionary error, severity 2: anatomy blurred, meaning recoverable.
[SUB:worse->yeah] — Annunciation/Homonym, severity 2: the course of the symptom is lost, but nothing false is stated.
[INS:alcohol] (the patient said it twice) — Human transcription error, severity 1.
[DEL:the] — Stop words, severity 1: no clinical content.

If you are stuck between 2 and 3, ask: would a clinician reading only this transcript believe something false? Yes → 3. No, they would just be missing something → 2.

5. Annotation Workflow

  1. Read: Review the human transcript (left) to understand context
  2. Compare: Review the ASR output (right) with highlighted errors
  3. Click: Click on a colored error to open the annotation modal
  4. Categorize from the text: Follow steps 1 to 4 of the chart (Dictionary, Suffix, Stop words, Nonsense). If one fits, stop. No audio needed
  5. Listen only if needed: If none of steps 1 to 4 fits, play the audio (it jumps to roughly where you clicked) and continue down the chart: Human transcription error, Audio glitch, Annunciation / Homonym, ASR omission / insertion. Pick one ASR Error Class (two only if two words failed for different reasons)
  6. Rate: Click Minor, Moderate, or Severe — nothing is pre-selected
  7. Save: Click "Save Annotation" - auto-syncs to server
  8. Continue: Move to next error until the session tracker shows all errors annotated

6. Best Practices

💡 Tips for Accuracy:
  • Review the human transcript carefully.
  • Text first, audio last. Label from the text when the chart allows it (steps 1 to 4). Play the audio only for the last four labels. Unclear audio is not evidence that the reference is wrong.
  • Consider clinical context for severity rating
  • Pick one label per damaged word
  • Be consistent in severity scoring
  • Take breaks every hour to avoid fatigue
💡 Tip: Annotated error Needs annotation

7. FAQ

Q: Can an error have multiple categories?

A: Only when the tag covers two or more damaged words that failed for different reasons. Otherwise pick one.

Q: What if I make a mistake?

A: Click the error again to re-open and modify your annotation.

Q: How is my progress saved?

A: Annotations auto-save to the server after each save. Your progress persists across sessions.
